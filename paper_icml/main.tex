\begin{abstract}
Adding modalities is widely assumed to improve predictive models. We study
multimodal sentiment classification on Amazon Reviews~2023, combining review
text (SBERT embeddings) with structured product/review metadata, and ask a
narrower question: \emph{when modalities disagree, does fusion still help?}
Using a fixed, balanced, leakage-controlled evaluation protocol with redrawn
splits across five seeds, we observe that early fusion improves average accuracy
by only a small but significant margin over a strong text-only baseline
($+0.5$ points overall, paired $t$-test $p{=}0.002$), while \emph{worsening} the
calibration gap between agreement and disagreement cases ($+0.069$, $p{=}0.002$).
On the disagreement subset itself, fusion provides no significant accuracy gain
($p{=}0.79$). We show the phenomenon persists across five product categories and
is not an artifact of product-level rating priors: removing leakage-prone
features (\texttt{product\_average\_rating}, \texttt{product\_rating\_number})
significantly lowers accuracy ($-0.8$ points, $p{=}0.024$), confirming those
features inflate results rather than explaining the conflict failure. We
introduce \textbf{UGCA-Fusion} (Uncertainty-Guided Conflict-Aware Fusion), a
learned method whose conflict detector estimates modality conflict at inference
\emph{without the true label} (AUROC ${\approx}0.72$). UGCA-Fusion's conflict
detector is genuinely predictive and its components ablate sensibly, but in our
setting it does not significantly beat early fusion or simple
disagreement-aware reweighting on disagreement accuracy. Our main claim is
deliberately cautious: \emph{average multimodal gains mask conflict-specific
reliability failures, and conflict-aware inference-time fusion can reduce, but
not eliminate, these failures.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Multimodal models are typically evaluated by average metrics, which can hide
systematic failures on instances where modalities provide conflicting evidence.
In product reviews, a glowing text paired with a low star rating (or vice versa)
is a concrete instance of \emph{modality conflict}. We ask whether the standard
practice of fusing text and metadata helps or hurts precisely on these conflict
cases, and whether any apparent benefit survives (i)~leakage control,
(ii)~multiple seeds, and (iii)~multiple product domains.

Our contributions, stated cautiously and supported by multi-seed evidence:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A systematic, leakage-controlled evaluation protocol for modality conflict
with a full conflict-aware metric suite (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
\item Evidence across five Amazon categories that fusion's average gains coincide
with a \emph{widening} agreement/disagreement calibration gap
(Sections~\ref{sec:main},~\ref{sec:cross}).
\item A leakage-controlled analysis showing the phenomenon is not caused by
product-level rating priors (Section~\ref{sec:main}).
\item \textbf{UGCA-Fusion}, a learned inference-time conflict-aware fusion method
with a label-free conflict detector (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item Calibration and statistical analyses, plus a human-annotation pipeline that
separates genuine conflict from label noise (Sections~\ref{sec:calib},
~\ref{sec:human}).
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit about negative results: UGCA-Fusion does not solve conflict, and
we report it as such.

\section{Related Work}
Multimodal fusion has a long history~\citep{baltrusaitis2018multimodal,
zadeh2017tensor}, with evidence that naively adding modalities can hurt
optimization and generalization~\citep{wang2020makes,peng2022balanced}.
Trusted/uncertainty-aware multi-view methods weight modalities by estimated
reliability~\citep{han2021trusted,kendall2018multitask}; our conflict detector is
related but targets \emph{instance-level} conflict inferred from unimodal
predictive distributions. Calibration of modern networks is
well-studied~\citep{guo2017calibration,naeini2015obtaining}, as is selective
prediction~\citep{geifman2017selective}; we apply these tools \emph{conditioned
on conflict}. For evaluation we use McNemar's test~\citep{dietterich1998approximate}
and bootstrap confidence intervals. Cross-modal sentiment benchmarks such as
CMU-MOSEI~\citep{zadeh2018mosei} and Hateful Memes~\citep{kiela2020hateful} are
natural future testbeds (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Problem Setup}\label{sec:setup}
We use Amazon Reviews~2023~\citep{hou2024amazon}. For each category we build a
fixed balanced pool of $20{,}000$ reviews (positive: stars~$\ge4$;
negative: stars~$\le2$; stars~$=3$ dropped), encode text with
SBERT (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2})~\citep{reimers2019sentence}, and form metadata
features. The pool is fixed with a master seed; only the stratified
$70/15/15$ train/val/test split is redrawn per run seed
($\{42,123,456,789,2026\}$), exposing split-level variance while keeping the
expensive encoding to a single CPU pass.

\paragraph{Leakage control.} The full metadata set includes product-level
aggregates (\texttt{product\_average\_rating}, \texttt{product\_rating\_number})
that can encode the review label. Our \emph{main} setting is
\textbf{leakage-controlled (LC)}: these aggregates are removed. We report
full-feature results only as a contrast.

\paragraph{Metric suite.} Beyond accuracy/F1/AUROC we report ECE, Brier score,
accuracy and ECE \emph{stratified} by agreement/disagreement/strong-disagreement,
the \emph{calibration gap} (disagreement ECE $-$ agreement ECE),
high-confidence error rate, confidence on correct vs.\ incorrect predictions,
and modality dominance. We use bootstrap $95\%$ CIs, paired bootstrap tests, and
McNemar's test.

\section{Disagreement and Conflict Definitions}\label{sec:defs}
Because ``disagreement'' is not uniquely defined, we compare four definitions on
the All\_Beauty pool using 5-fold cross-fitted (leakage-free) predictions:
\textbf{(A)} pretrained-sentiment (DistilBERT-SST-2~\citep{sanh2019distilbert})
vs.\ rating label; \textbf{(B)} cross-model disagreement between text-only and
metadata-only (LC) predictions; \textbf{(C)} high-confidence text contradiction
(text confidence $\ge0.90$ and text prediction $\neq$ label); and \textbf{(D)} a
200-example human-annotation subset.

Table~\ref{tab:defs} shows the definitions flag \emph{largely disjoint}
instances (pairwise Jaccard $\le0.11$), so the choice of definition materially
changes conclusions. Definition~A reproduces a large multimodal failure
(accuracy $0.64$ on flagged vs.\ $0.91$ elsewhere); Definition~C is rare but the
model is almost always wrong on it ($0.008$), consistent with label noise /
sarcasm---motivating human annotation (Section~\ref{sec:human}). Unless noted,
the rest of the paper uses Definition~A.

\section{UGCA-Fusion}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Uncertainty-Guided Conflict-Aware Fusion} aims to know, at inference and
without the true label, when modalities conflict, and to route fusion
accordingly. Text and metadata encoders produce $h_{\text{text}}, h_{\text{meta}}$
and unimodal heads produce $p_{\text{text}}, p_{\text{meta}}$. A conflict
detector predicts $\hat{c}$ from conflict features:
$|p_{\text{text}}-p_{\text{meta}}|$, the entropies of $p_{\text{text}}$ and
$p_{\text{meta}}$, and the Jensen--Shannon divergence~\citep{lin1991divergence}
between the unimodal distributions (plus optional learned features). A gate $g$,
modulated by $\hat{c}$, forms
$h_{\text{fused}} = g\,h_{\text{text}} + (1-g)\,h_{\text{meta}}$, and a classifier
predicts $\hat{y}$. The training loss is
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L} =\ & \mathrm{CE}(\hat y, y)
+ \lambda_{\text{t}}\mathrm{CE}(p_{\text{text}}, y)
+ \lambda_{\text{m}}\mathrm{CE}(p_{\text{meta}}, y)\\
&+ \lambda_{\text{c}}\,\mathrm{BCE}(\hat c, d)
+ \lambda_{\text{cal}}\,\Omega_{\text{cal}},
\end{align*}
where $d$ is the proxy disagreement label (used \emph{only in training}) and
$\Omega_{\text{cal}}$ is a differentiable calibration surrogate. At inference the
detector uses no label, which is the core claim we test empirically.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:exp}
Text-only is SBERT${+}$logistic regression; metadata-only is XGBoost; fusion
models are MLPs (early/late/gated). Disagreement-aware reweighting upweights
training disagreement rows ($w{=}5$). UGCA-Fusion and four ablations
(no conflict detector, no entropy, no JS, no calibration loss) train for up to
25 epochs with early stopping. All models share each seed's split. Compute is
CPU-only; this constrains the second-dataset scope (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Main Results}\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the canonical All\_Beauty results (mean$\pm$std over
five seeds; pool disagreement rate $11.6\%$). Three observations hold:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fusion's overall gain is small.} Early-fusion (LC) accuracy
$0.881$ vs.\ text-only $0.876$ ($+0.005$; paired $t$-test $p{=}0.002$, Cohen's
$d_z{=}3.2$). On the disagreement subset the gain is not significant
($+0.002$, $p{=}0.79$).
\item \textbf{Fusion worsens conflict calibration.} The calibration gap rises
from $0.120$ (text-only) to $0.189$ (early-fusion LC); the per-seed paired
increase is $+0.069$ ($p{=}0.002$, $d_z{=}3.2$). Gated/late fusion improve
\emph{average} ECE but the conflict gap remains elevated.
\item \textbf{Not explained by leakage.} Full-feature early fusion beats LC by
$+0.8$ points accuracy ($p{=}0.024$) and $+2.4$ points disagreement accuracy
($p{=}0.011$): the product-rating aggregates inflate results, so the LC setting
(our main one) is the honest baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Cross-Domain Generalization}\label{sec:cross}
Across five categories (All\_Beauty, Appliances, Digital\_Music, Gift\_Cards,
Video\_Games; seeds $\{42,123,456\}$), early fusion (LC) consistently increases
\emph{both} average accuracy \emph{and} the calibration gap relative to text-only
(Table~\ref{tab:cross}). Pooling over category$\times$seed, the accuracy gain is
$+0.7$ points ($p{=}0.0006$) and the disagreement-accuracy gain is modest
($+2.4$ points, $p{=}0.043$); notably the disagreement effect is inconsistent
in sign (e.g.\ Video\_Games: $0.586$ vs.\ $0.603$). The calibration-gap increase,
by contrast, is consistent across all five categories. We therefore claim
replication for the \emph{calibration} failure but only weak, domain-dependent
support for a disagreement-accuracy benefit.

\section{Calibration, Selective Prediction, and UGCA Analysis}\label{sec:calib}
The conflict-aware metric suite shows fusion concentrates its calibration error
on conflict cases. UGCA-Fusion's \emph{conflict detector} predicts the proxy
disagreement label on the test set with AUROC ${\approx}0.72$ (mean over 5 seeds)
\emph{using no true label}, supporting the core hypothesis that conflict is
detectable at inference. Ablations behave as designed: removing the conflict
detector lowers disagreement accuracy ($0.658{\to}0.646$) and removing the
calibration loss worsens the calibration gap ($0.206{\to}0.221$). However,
across five seeds UGCA-Fusion is statistically indistinguishable from early
fusion on accuracy and on the disagreement subset (paired $p{>}0.7$;
example-level McNemar median $p{>}0.5$), and gated fusion remains best-calibrated
(ECE $0.018$). UGCA-Fusion does significantly beat disagreement-aware reweighting
on \emph{overall} accuracy ($+1.6$ points, $p{=}0.001$), as reweighting trades
overall accuracy for disagreement accuracy.

\section{Human Annotation / Qualitative Analysis}\label{sec:human}
We provide a reproducible human-annotation pipeline: a six-category guide
(genuine conflict, label noise, mixed sentiment, sarcasm, unclear,
metadata-prior), a batch generator, and an analyzer computing the
genuine-conflict vs.\ label-noise breakdown, Cohen's $\kappa$ inter-annotator
agreement, and model accuracy per human category. We export 200 cross-fitted
disagreement candidates for labeling. We \emph{do not} fabricate labels; the
analyzer reports ``annotation pending'' until human files exist. This separates
genuine model failures from dataset artifacts and is a stated prerequisite for
strong claims about ``conflict.''

\section{Ablations}\label{sec:abl}
See Section~\ref{sec:calib} and \texttt{reports\_icml/tables/ugca\_ablation.csv}.
Each UGCA component (conflict detector, entropy features, JS divergence,
calibration loss) contributes in the expected direction, but the marginal effects
are small relative to seed variance.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
(1)~The disagreement label is a \emph{proxy}; Section~\ref{sec:defs} shows
definitions disagree, and human annotation is pending. (2)~Both evaluated
families are review${+}$metadata; a second \emph{family} is scoped (Yelp;
feasibility report included) and a true cross-modality test (CMU-MOSEI, Hateful
Memes) is GPU-required future work. (3)~Compute is CPU-only, so encoders are
frozen and pools are capped at $20$k. (4)~With five seeds, $p$-values are
interpreted cautiously alongside effect sizes and CIs. (5)~UGCA-Fusion is a
diagnostic advance, not an accuracy solution.

\section{Conclusion}
Across the evaluated categories we observe that average multimodal gains mask
conflict-specific reliability failures: fusion barely improves overall accuracy,
does not reliably help on disagreement cases, and significantly worsens the
agreement/disagreement calibration gap, even after leakage control. Our
UGCA-Fusion can \emph{detect} conflict at inference without labels but does not
eliminate the failure. We therefore frame robust multimodal behavior under
modality conflict as an open problem, and release a leakage-controlled,
multi-seed, multi-category evaluation protocol to study it.
