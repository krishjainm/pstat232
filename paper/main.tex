\begin{abstract}
Multimodal learning integrates diverse data sources under the assumption that combining modalities improves prediction, yet real-world inputs frequently contain conflicting cross-modal signals.
That modalities can disagree, and that fusion models can over-rely on one modality, is already known; our contribution is a \emph{systematic, statistically grounded characterization} of \emph{when} and \emph{how} this conflict degrades a text--metadata sentiment classifier, together with a simple mitigation.
Using the Amazon Reviews 2023 dataset across three product categories (All Beauty, Digital Music, Appliances), we build text (sentence-transformer), metadata (XGBoost), and three fusion models (early, late, gated), then evaluate them on agreement and graded-disagreement subsets with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals and McNemar's tests.
Our analysis shows that: (1) fusion raises average accuracy (88.8\% vs.\ 86.8\% text-only) but drops to 64.1\% on disagreement cases; (2) the fusion model resolves true text--metadata conflicts by following text 82.2\% of the time; (3) disagreement induces a 13$\times$ calibration gap (ECE 0.219 vs.\ 0.017) that a single global temperature cannot fix; and (4) early, late, and gated fusion behave near-identically, indicating the failure is not specific to a single fusion architecture.
Finally, we compare simple mitigations: \textbf{disagreement-aware loss reweighting} improves disagreement accuracy by up to 3.2~pp (F1 by 4.3~pp) for a small overall cost, while focal loss and temperature scaling improve calibration but not disagreement accuracy---no single intervention fixes both, and the gains are modest relative to training-seed variance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multimodal machine learning combines heterogeneous data sources---text, images, structured metadata, and other signals---to improve predictions \citep{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}.
A common assumption is that more modalities always help: more information should yield better decisions.
In practice, however, modalities can conflict.
A product review's text may sound enthusiastic while its star rating is low, or metadata features (price, helpfulness votes) may suggest a pattern inconsistent with the textual content.

When modalities disagree, fusion models face a fundamental dilemma: which signal should dominate?
Prior work has established that multimodal models can over-rely on a single modality \citep{wang2020makes, peng2022balanced} and that modality gaps in representation space hinder effective fusion \citep{liang2022mind}.
We take these phenomena as our starting point rather than our finding: our goal is not to show \emph{that} modalities disagree, but to systematically characterize \emph{when} fusion degrades, \emph{how} it resolves conflicting inputs, how this interacts with calibration, and whether a simple training-time intervention helps---all with explicit statistical uncertainty.

Concretely, this paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Graded disagreement evaluation.} We operationalize text--rating conflict into four severity subgroups (agreement, weak/medium/strong disagreement) and report performance per subgroup with bootstrap 95\% CIs and McNemar's tests, making the agreement$\to$disagreement degradation and its uncertainty explicit.
  \item \textbf{Dominance quantification.} We measure modality reliance with three complementary lenses---label-based, probability-based, and true-conflict dominance---showing fusion consistently follows text under conflict (68--82\% across categories).
  \item \textbf{Group-conditional calibration.} We show a 13$\times$ ECE gap between agreement and disagreement and that a single global temperature cannot close it, motivating modality-conditioned calibration.
  \item \textbf{An honest comparison of simple mitigations.} We compare disagreement reweighting (fixed and severity-aware), focal loss, and post-hoc temperature scaling, reporting the full accuracy--calibration trade-off rather than a single best number. Reweighting buys disagreement accuracy at small overall cost; focal loss and temperature scaling improve calibration but not disagreement accuracy; no method improves both.
  \item \textbf{Robustness and a leakage audit.} We replicate across three product categories and three fusion architectures, vary training seeds, ablate the text encoder, and audit metadata leakage---finding that the metadata model's skill is largely driven by the product-level \texttt{product\_average\_rating} feature, which we therefore treat cautiously.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Multimodal Learning.}
\citet{baltrusaitis2019multimodal} survey multimodal ML, categorizing fusion into early, late, and hybrid approaches.
While theory shows that, under benign conditions, multiple modalities provably outperform any single one \citep{huang2021makes}, such guarantees assume modalities are mutually informative; how fusion behaves when modalities actively conflict remains underexplored.

\paragraph{Modality Dominance and Bias.}
\citet{wang2020makes} show that multimodal networks often converge to relying on one modality due to learning dynamics.
\citet{peng2022balanced} propose gradient modulation to address this.
In VQA, language bias causes models to ignore visual evidence \citep{goyal2017making}.
Our work quantifies dominance specifically under modality conflict.

\paragraph{Modality Gap.}
\citet{liang2022mind} identify a ``modality gap'' where different modalities occupy distinct embedding regions, hindering integration under conflicting signals.

\paragraph{Calibration.}
\citet{guo2017calibration} show that modern networks are poorly calibrated.
\citet{nixon2019measuring} formalize ECE.
We extend calibration analysis to agreement/disagreement subgroups.

\paragraph{Sentence Representations.}
We use sentence-transformer embeddings \citep{reimers2019sentence} as our text representation, which provide dense 384-dimensional vectors encoding semantic similarity.

\paragraph{Robustness and Failure Analysis.}
Subgroup evaluation is standard for fairness \citep{barocas2019fairness}; our application to multimodal conflict analysis is novel.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{x}^{(t)}, \mathbf{x}^{(m)})$ denote an input with text modality $\mathbf{x}^{(t)}$ and metadata modality $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}$, with true label $y \in \{0, 1\}$.

We train three classifiers: text-only $f_t$, metadata-only $f_m$, and multimodal $f_{mm}$.
We define \textbf{modality disagreement} using an external sentiment classifier $g$: when $g(\mathbf{x}^{(t)}) \neq y$, text sentiment contradicts the rating-derived label.
We stratify by $g$'s confidence $c_g$ into weak ($c_g < 0.70$), medium ($0.70 \leq c_g < 0.90$), and strong ($c_g \geq 0.90$) disagreement.

\paragraph{Probability-Based Dominance.}
For each sample, we compute:
\begin{equation}
  r = \frac{|p_{mm} - p_t|}{|p_{mm} - p_t| + |p_{mm} - p_m| + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
where $p_{mm}$, $p_t$, $p_m$ are predicted $P(y=1)$.
$r \approx 0$ indicates text dominance; $r \approx 1$ indicates metadata dominance.

\section{Data}

We use the Amazon Reviews 2023 dataset \citep{hou2024bridging}, constructing binary sentiment tasks.
Reviews with rating $\geq 4$ are positive; $\leq 2$ are negative; neutrals are excluded.

\paragraph{Class Balancing.}
The raw dataset is 77.5\% positive.
We downsample the majority class to match the minority, yielding 20{,}674 balanced samples (10{,}337 per class).
This ensures the majority-class baseline achieves exactly 50\% accuracy.

\paragraph{Modalities.}
The \textbf{text modality} is encoded via \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} sentence-transformer \citep{reimers2019sentence} into 384 dimensions.
The \textbf{metadata modality} comprises eight features: review length (words/characters), helpful votes, verified purchase, product average rating, product rating count, product price, and review year.

\paragraph{Splits and Disagreement.}
We use 70/15/15 stratified splits (seed 42): 14{,}471 train, 3{,}101 val, 3{,}102 test.
A pretrained DistilBERT sentiment classifier labels 11.1\% of test samples as disagreement (343 of 3{,}102), with 27 weak, 50 medium, and 266 strong.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Models}

\paragraph{Text-Only.}
Sentence-transformer embeddings fed into logistic regression ($C=1.0$).

\paragraph{Metadata-Only.}
XGBoost (200 estimators, max depth 6) on standardized features.

\paragraph{Multimodal Fusion.}
Three architectures:
\textbf{Early fusion} concatenates text (384-d) and metadata (8-d), then applies a 2-layer MLP (256$\to$64$\to$2) with ReLU and dropout (0.2).
\textbf{Late fusion} uses separate per-modality MLPs and averages logits.
\textbf{Gated fusion} learns a sigmoid gate over modality representations.
All trained for 15 epochs with Adam (lr=$10^{-3}$, batch size 64).
We use default hyperparameters throughout, as our focus is on comparing model behavior under disagreement rather than maximizing absolute performance.

\subsection{Disagreement-Aware Training}

Standard fusion training treats all samples equally, but disagreement samples represent qualitatively different inputs.
We propose \textbf{disagreement-aware loss reweighting}: during training, disagreement samples receive weight $w > 1$ in the cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \ell(f_{mm}(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i), \quad w_i = \begin{cases} w & \text{if disagreement} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This encourages the model to pay more attention to conflict cases without modifying the architecture.
We evaluate $w \in \{3, 5\}$.

\subsection{Evaluation}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Accuracy, F1, AUROC, ECE, and Brier score, all with 95\% bootstrap CIs (1{,}000 resamples).

\paragraph{Statistical Tests.}
McNemar's test (continuity-corrected) for pairwise comparisons.

\paragraph{Calibration.}
ECE overall and by agreement group.
Temperature scaling learned on validation data.

\paragraph{Selective Prediction.}
Accuracy--coverage curves at varying confidence thresholds (Figure~\ref{fig:selective}).

\subsection{Statistical Significance}

On the overall test set, all pairwise differences are significant (Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar}).
On the disagreement subset, multimodal vs.\ text-only is significant ($p = 0.031$), but metadata vs.\ multimodal ($p = 0.456$) and text vs.\ metadata ($p = 0.568$) are not---model differences largely vanish under conflict.
Restricting further to the \emph{strong}-disagreement subset ($n = 266$), even the multimodal-vs.-text-only advantage is no longer significant (continuity-corrected McNemar $\chi^2 = 2.22$, $p = 0.136$; paired bootstrap 95\% CI on the accuracy gap $[-0.090, +0.008]$), and multimodal vs.\ metadata-only is likewise indistinguishable ($p = 0.534$). In our setting, the apparent benefit of fusion thus concentrates in the agreement regime and erodes precisely where modalities conflict most. We report these extended paired comparisons in \texttt{statistical\_tests\_extended.csv}.

\subsection{Comparing Mitigation Strategies}

To understand the space of simple interventions---and to test whether the mitigation transfers beyond a single domain---we compare four families on a second category (Appliances), using identical features and the early-fusion backbone (Table~\ref{tab:mitigation}): fixed disagreement reweighting ($w\in\{2,3,5,8\}$), severity-aware reweighting (weights $1/2/4/6$ for agreement/weak/medium/strong), focal loss ($\gamma\in\{1,2\}$, optionally combined with reweighting), and post-hoc temperature scaling (global vs.\ group-wise).
We deliberately report the full trade-off rather than selecting a single ``winner.''
We observe a clear division of labor: \textbf{reweighting methods} (especially severity-aware) buy disagreement and strong-disagreement \emph{accuracy} at a small overall-accuracy cost but do not improve calibration, whereas \textbf{focal loss and temperature scaling} substantially improve \emph{calibration} (overall ECE) but leave disagreement accuracy---and the high disagreement-subset ECE---essentially unchanged.
No single method improves accuracy and calibration simultaneously.
We also note honestly that on this category the smallest fixed weights ($w=2,3$) did \emph{not} help disagreement accuracy, unlike the All Beauty result above; the effective intervention here is severity-aware weighting.

\paragraph{Robustness across seeds.}
Re-training the fusion model under three seeds (42/123/456) on the Appliances split (varying initialization and minibatch order, with the split and features held fixed), severity/reweighting gains are real but modest relative to training noise: Standard reaches $.776\pm.016$ disagreement accuracy vs.\ $.790\pm.015$ for DA ($w{=}5$), and $.752\pm.021$ vs.\ $.773\pm.013$ on strong disagreement.
We therefore frame the mitigations as a favorable but small shift along the overall--disagreement trade-off, not as a decisive fix (full numbers in \texttt{multiseed\_results.csv}).

\subsection{Ablation and Leakage Audit}

\paragraph{Is the metadata model leaking the label?}
The strongest metadata feature, \texttt{product\_average\_rating}, is a \emph{product-level} aggregate of ratings and is correlated with the per-review label (point-biserial $r = 0.38$ on All Beauty).
A 5-fold cross-validation audit on the All Beauty test partition shows it carries most of the metadata model's signal: metadata accuracy falls from $.66$ (eight features) to $.55$ when \texttt{product\_average\_rating} is removed, and to $.55$ when all product-level features are removed (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
We flag this as a meaningful leakage risk and designate the \emph{no-\texttt{product\_average\_rating}} configuration as the more conservative metadata setting.
Crucially, our central disagreement claims concern \emph{multimodal vs.\ text} behavior under conflict and the dominance of text, neither of which depends on this feature.

\paragraph{Text encoder and fusion architecture.}
On the Appliances split, TF--IDF + logistic regression is competitive with (indeed marginally above) sentence-transformer embeddings for text-only accuracy ($.880$ vs.\ $.870$), suggesting that for this task the disagreement phenomenon is not an artifact of the specific text encoder.
As reported earlier (Table~\ref{tab:fusion}), early/late/gated fusion are within $0.6$~pp of one another.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Does Multimodal Always Help?}
On average, yes---but the 19--28~pp accuracy drop from agreement to disagreement reveals that aggregate metrics mask important reliability failures across all three product categories.
On strong disagreement, the multimodal model can even underperform metadata-only (e.g., 58.6\% vs.\ 61.3\% in All Beauty), suggesting text dominance becomes harmful when text is misleading.

\paragraph{Fusion Architecture Has Little Effect.}
Early, late, and gated fusion achieve 88.3--88.9\% accuracy with nearly identical disagreement performance, and all three learn similar text-dominant strategies.
Within the standard fusion designs we test, this suggests effort is better spent on conflict-aware training objectives than on swapping among these architectures.

\paragraph{Mitigation Helps, but Modestly.}
Disagreement-aware reweighting shifts the model along the overall--disagreement trade-off in a favorable direction (e.g.\ $+3.2$~pp disagreement accuracy on All Beauty for a $1.5$~pp overall cost; severity-aware weighting gives the largest disagreement gains on Appliances).
However, our multi-seed runs show these gains are modest relative to training noise, and our method comparison shows that accuracy-oriented and calibration-oriented interventions address different failures: reweighting helps disagreement \emph{accuracy} while focal loss / temperature scaling help \emph{calibration}, with neither closing the disagreement-subset ECE gap.
We therefore present these as partial, complementary mitigations rather than a solution---reliability under conflict remains an open problem.

\paragraph{Cross-Category Consistency.}
The replication across three product categories (All Beauty, Digital Music, Appliances) strengthens our claims.
Disagreement rates (10--16\%), performance degradation patterns, and text dominance (68--82\%) are all consistent, suggesting these are general properties of text--metadata fusion rather than artifacts of a single domain.

\paragraph{Calibration Under Conflict.}
The 13$\times$ ECE gap and temperature scaling's failure motivate group-conditional calibration.
In production, systems should flag high-confidence predictions in detected disagreement regions.

\paragraph{Practical Implications.}
We recommend:
(1) monitoring disagreement rates, as they predict reliability;
(2) applying disagreement-aware training when conflict cases matter;
(3) using selective prediction with higher thresholds for detected disagreement;
(4) developing modality-conditioned calibration methods.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Scope.} All experiments use the Amazon Reviews 2023 text--metadata setting. We do not claim our quantitative findings transfer to other multimodal regimes (e.g.\ vision--language); the methodology, not the specific numbers, is what we expect to generalize.
  \item \textbf{Disagreement is defined via a proxy.} Both the rating-derived labels and the pretrained DistilBERT sentiment model are imperfect. ``Disagreement'' therefore partly reflects errors of the labeling pipeline, and the disagreement subset is enriched for genuinely hard or mislabeled reviews. Our claims concern behavior on this operationalized subset, not a ground-truth notion of semantic conflict.
  \item \textbf{Small subgroups.} The weak ($n{=}27$) and medium ($n{=}50$) disagreement subgroups yield wide confidence intervals; per-subgroup conclusions for these bins are tentative and reported only alongside their CIs.
  \item \textbf{Single split for headline numbers.} Main results use one stratified test split (seed 42); the cross-category replication mitigates but does not replace a full multi-seed study of the mitigation effect.
  \item \textbf{Limited metadata.} The metadata modality is eight structured tabular features; richer modalities (images, audio, video) may exhibit different disagreement dynamics.
  \item \textbf{Heuristic taxonomy.} The error taxonomy uses keyword/pattern heuristics with a large ``other'' bucket (46\%); manual annotation would be needed to make its category rates authoritative.
  \item \textbf{Train-time labels required.} Disagreement-aware training needs disagreement labels during training; we do not address detecting conflict at inference time on unseen distributions.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete category sweep.} One attempted category (Video Games) failed due to a metadata parsing issue, so cross-category claims rest on three of four categories.
  \item \textbf{Metadata leakage.} The metadata model's modest skill is largely attributable to \texttt{product\_average\_rating}, a product-level aggregate that partially encodes the target (removing it drops metadata CV accuracy from $.66$ to $.55$). We treat the no-\texttt{product\_average\_rating} setting as the conservative one; ``metadata-only'' difficulty should be read with this caveat.
  \item \textbf{Mitigation evidence is partial.} The extended mitigation comparison and multi-seed robustness study were run on the Appliances split (the materialized data on hand); gains there are modest relative to training-seed variance, and we vary only training stochasticity (not the data split). A full multi-seed, multi-category mitigation study remains future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that multimodal sentiment classifiers exhibit systematic failure modes under modality conflict.
Across three product categories (All Beauty, Digital Music, Appliances), the multimodal fusion model achieves 88.8--93.1\% overall accuracy but drops to 64.1--74.5\% on disagreement cases---a consistent 19--25~pp degradation.
Text dominance ranges from 68--82\%, all three fusion architectures perform comparably, and standard calibration fails to address the 13$\times$ ECE gap between agreement and disagreement subgroups.

We further show that simple interventions partially mitigate the problem---disagreement-aware reweighting improves disagreement F1 by up to 4.3~pp, and severity-aware weighting gives the largest disagreement-accuracy gains---while our method comparison and multi-seed runs make clear these gains are modest and that no single intervention fixes both accuracy and calibration under conflict.
Future directions include:
(1) modality dropout and stochastic gating;
(2) uncertainty-based fusion with per-sample reliability estimates;
(3) modality-conditioned calibration;
and (4) disagreement detection at inference time without ground-truth labels.
